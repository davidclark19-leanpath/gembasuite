Where does the time go?

Two tools for looking at your own histopathology service: a specimen journey map for finding where work waits, and a turnaround tracker that shows whether your current system can meet its target. About fifteen minutes to run as a group session.

✓ Everything runs in your browser. No patient data is used or stored, and nothing is sent anywhere.

New to these ideas? Why histology queues grow explains why work waits, and Demand and capacity shows what leave and sickness do to a team's real capacity. Both make the tracker easier to read.

The specimen journey, and where waits hide

Turnaround is almost all waiting, not work. A specimen typically spends a few hours being worked on across a journey of several days. Use this map as a starting point for a value stream walk: record the actual work time and wait time at each step in your own lab.

#StepTypeWhere waits commonly hide

Work and wait times in the template are left blank on purpose. Fill them from observation, not from memory.

Turnaround against your target

Paste your weekly percentage of cases reported within 10 days, oldest first. Twenty or more weeks gives a useful picture. The chart shows the natural process limits: the range of routine week-to-week variation your current system produces, if nothing changes in how it works.

One percentage per line, oldest week first. Weeks are numbered automatically, so nothing else can be typed in. Aggregated figures only.

For facilitators: a 15-minute running order
  1. 0 to 3 minutes. Frame the question: "A specimen is worked on for a few hours across a journey of several days. Where does the rest of the time go?"
  2. 3 to 7 minutes. Walk the specimen journey table. Ask which steps are batches and which are queues in the room's own labs, and where they think the longest wait is.
  3. 7 to 11 minutes. Load the illustrative data in the tracker. Read the limits and the verdict together: a target above the upper limit is a design problem, not an effort problem.
  4. 11 to 13 minutes. If someone has brought real weekly figures, paste them in and discuss which points are signals and which are ordinary noise.
  5. 13 to 15 minutes. The ask: before the next meeting, record actual work and wait times at three steps of your own journey using the template, and plot your last 20 or more weeks of turnaround.

The tracker uses an XmR chart (natural process limits at the mean plus or minus 2.66 times the average moving range). It is a pragmatic choice for weekly percentages. Where weekly case numbers are small or vary a lot, a p-chart, which allows for the number of cases each week, is usually better, though it has assumptions of its own. Twenty weeks is a small baseline; treat the limits as estimates.
